Can my HOA stop me from installing artificial turf in Florida?

By Jereme Strange, AJ Turf · years on the manufacturing side of the turf industry, and years installing and repairing turf in Florida

The short answer

For a back yard your HOA can't see, no: Florida law (§720.3045) bars an HOA from restricting turf that isn't visible from the street, a neighbor's parcel, a common area, or a golf course. A visible front yard is different — your HOA keeps its architectural-review say there, even though the city or county can no longer ban it.

The short version

Your situation Who could object What the law says
Back yard / any turf not visible from the street, an adjacent parcel, common area, or golf course Your HOA §720.3045: the HOA may not restrict it. Protected by state statute
Front yard visible from the street Your HOA HOA architectural review still applies — the statute does not protect visible turf
Any single-family lot of 1 acre or less Your city or county §125.572 + DEP Rule 62-308: local government can't ban turf, or regulate it inconsistently with the state standard, on lots that meet that standard
Condo, duplex, multi-family, commercial, or a lot over 1 acre Local government The §125.572 preemption doesn't reach these — local rules can still apply

Two laws, two different governments, two different answers. Most of the confusion in Florida comes from treating them as one.

What does §720.3045 actually say? (the HOA law)

Florida Statute §720.3045 says an HOA may not restrict artificial turf — it names turf alongside things like boats and gardens — that is not visible from the parcel's frontage, an adjacent parcel, a common area, or a community golf course.

Read it for what it is: a visibility rule. If the turf can't be seen from any of those four vantage points, your HOA's restriction doesn't stand — the back yard behind your fence is protected by statute. If the turf can be seen — a front yard facing the street is the obvious case — the statute doesn't cover it, and your HOA keeps the architectural-review authority it has over other visible changes to your home.

One distinction worth knowing: Florida-friendly landscaping has its own separate statutory protection (§720.3075(4), tied to §373.185). Homeowners sometimes cite it for turf. It's a different provision about a different thing — turf is governed by §720.3045 and §125.572, and mixing the arguments up doesn't strengthen either one.

What changed with §125.572 and Rule 62-308? (the city/county law)

The second law is about your city and county, not your HOA. In 2025, Florida passed HB 683, whose turf section — codified at §125.572 — directed the state DEP to adopt minimum installation standards for synthetic turf on single-family residential lots of 1 acre or less, and provided that once those standards existed, local governments could no longer ban turf or regulate it inconsistently with them.

DEP's Rule 62-308.100 took effect May 19, 2026, and it is in force. So today: on a single-family lot of an acre or less, a city or county cannot ban artificial turf that meets the state standard. What local governments can still do is administer compliance — at least one Florida city has added a permit-review process to check turf installs against the rule, which is allowed precisely because reviewing compliance isn't prohibiting turf. Expect more of that, not less.

What does "installed to the state standard" mean?

Rule 62-308's standards, in plain terms:

  • 10-foot setback from natural or man-made water bodies.
  • Turf, backing and infill must not contain heavy metals or intentionally added PFAS.
  • The turf must be permeable: a permeable backing over a pervious base, graded for drainage, with no pooling or extra runoff onto neighbors.
  • The native soil under the base must not be compacted so hard that water can't soak through it.
  • No in-ground irrigation used to water synthetic turf.

That last one is why a proper install caps the sprinkler heads inside the turf footprint instead of removing the system — capped heads are both the compliant configuration and the reversible one; heads outside the turf that serve planting beds stay live. And the scope matters: the preemption covers single-family lots of 1 acre or less. Condos, duplexes, multi-family, commercial properties, and larger lots remain locally regulable.

So can my HOA still say no to a front yard?

Yes — that part of the headline version is wrong. The practical answer, the way we give it at quote time: "Back yard? State law says your HOA can't stop you. Front yard? The city can't ban it anymore if it's installed to the state standard — but your HOA can still have an architectural review, so let's check your HOA docs before we design it."

That two-step is the whole game. A front-yard project isn't illegal and isn't banned — it's reviewable. Many HOAs approve turf routinely when the submittal comes with product specs and install details in front of them, which is why the paperwork step belongs at the start of the project, not after the check is written.

Florida note — Southwest Florida specifics

As of this writing, no turf-specific installation permit requirement has been found in Lee County, Fort Myers, or Cape Coral — but permits can still attach to adjacent work (hardscape in a right-of-way, drainage tie-ins, pool-deck changes). Canal-front homes — a big share of Cape Coral and Fort Myers — add one more piece of math: the state's 10-foot waterbody setback has to be run before design, not discovered after. If your lot touches a canal or lake, that's a quote-time conversation.

The honest downside The statutes are narrower than the headlines. §720.3045 protects only turf nobody can see; the moment your project is a visible front yard, your HOA's architectural committee is fully in the picture, and "the state allows turf" is not an approval. The city/county preemption covers single-family lots of an acre or less — condo owners and larger-lot owners don't get its protection. And nothing in either law stops an HOA from enforcing its rules on visible work, or a city from checking that your install meets the state standard. This page is operational guidance from an installer who works under these rules, not legal advice — if your HOA is already threatening fines, that's a Florida attorney conversation.

The gotcha The sentence going around Florida — "the state banned HOAs from stopping turf" — is false for the exact project most people mean by it: a visible front yard. What's true is narrower and, used right, stronger: your city can't ban it (§125.572), and your HOA can't touch what it can't see (§720.3045). Installers who quote you the headline version either haven't read the statutes or are hoping your HOA fight becomes your problem after they've been paid. Get the two laws separated before you sign anything.

Questions people ask.

My HOA documents say no synthetic materials. Does the statute override that for my back yard?

For turf that isn't visible from the frontage, an adjacent parcel, a common area, or a community golf course, §720.3045 says the HOA may not restrict it — that's a state statute speaking directly to HOA restrictions. For anything visible, the documents and the architectural review still govern. When in doubt, the visibility question is the first one to answer.

Does the city's inability to ban turf cover my condo or townhome?

No. The §125.572 preemption covers single-family residential lots of 1 acre or less that meet the DEP standards. Condos, duplexes, multi-family, and commercial properties remain subject to local regulation, and your association's own rules are a separate layer on top of that.

Do I need a permit to install turf in Fort Myers or Cape Coral?

No turf-specific installation permit was found in Lee County, Fort Myers, or Cape Coral in our research — though permits can attach to adjacent work like hardscape or drainage changes, and some Florida cities elsewhere have begun adding DEP-compliance permit reviews. We check the specifics for your address at the estimate.

Does turf count as Florida-friendly landscaping under the other statute?

Different law. §720.3075(4) protects Florida-friendly landscaping as defined in §373.185; artificial turf is addressed by §720.3045 (HOAs) and §125.572 / Rule 62-308 (local governments). Citing the landscaping provision for a turf project mixes two arguments that regulators and HOAs treat separately.
