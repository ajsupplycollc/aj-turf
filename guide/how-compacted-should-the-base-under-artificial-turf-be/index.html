How compacted should the base under turf be?

By Jereme Strange, AJ Turf · years on the manufacturing side of the turf industry, and years installing and repairing turf in Florida

The short answer

The base under artificial turf should be compacted to 95% Standard Proctor, tested under ASTM D698 — the benchmark the Synthetic Turf Council publishes for synthetic turf base materials. In the field that feels like this: wet screening sand, compacted in lifts, until the sand doesn't move underfoot. Florida law itself sets no percentage.

What "95% Standard Proctor" actually means

A Proctor test is how engineers put a number on compaction. In the lab, ASTM D698 establishes the maximum dry density a material reaches under a standard compactive effort. Ninety-five percent of that density, achieved in the field, is 95% Standard Proctor — a firm, locked-together base that has given up nearly all the settling it is ever going to do.

The figure AJ Turf builds to is not our invention and not a Florida invention. It is the Synthetic Turf Council's published guideline for synthetic turf base materials: a minimum of 95% Standard Proctor. We adopted it as our written spec because it is the strongest citable standard in the industry — a number with a standard, a test method, and a named body behind it. AJ Turf aligns with Synthetic Turf Council guidance wherever it does not conflict with how we build.

In the field, the spec has a plain-language twin, and every good installer knows it: compact until the sand doesn't move. Wet the screening sand, compact it in lifts, and keep going until the surface is firm and non-yielding underfoot. The percentage is the written standard; the unmoving sand is what it looks like on your lot.

Why compaction decides whether an install lasts

An under-compacted base does not fail on install day. It fails on a schedule: traffic lanes settle, low spots appear, water starts sitting in the dips it created, and seams and edges get stressed by a surface that is quietly changing shape underneath them. Poor compaction leading to washout and divots is the failure chain behind a large share of the ripples, lumps, and puddling we are called out to repair.

Compaction is also a warranty issue, not just a quality issue. The manufacturer warranty excludes wear and abrasion from an inadequate sub-base, and excludes faulty installation outright. A base built soft does not only risk the yard's looks — it can hole the customer's product claim before the product itself ever fails.

The reverse error exists too, and it matters in Florida: compaction and permeability are in tension. Crush a base's pore space completely and water stops moving through it. The Synthetic Turf Council's own base-construction guidance makes the same judgment for open-graded stone — compaction stops once sufficient stability is attained. AJ Turf's base is screening sand, not open-graded stone: sand compacts measurably and predictably, which is exactly why a Proctor-referenced target is achievable on it. The goal is a base that is both locked and draining, not a parking lot.

The layer the percentage does not apply to

The 95% figure applies to the sand base we build — not to your native soil underneath it. Florida's statewide synthetic turf standard says so in its own words, in substance: the soil beneath the installed subgrade shall not be compacted to the extent that it adversely impacts percolation through the soil. That distinction is built into the AJ Method. We compact the base hard, in lifts, to spec — and we leave the native soil below it able to take water. Over-compacting the ground itself would trade a firm yard for a flooded one.

What Florida law does — and does not — specify

Be precise here, because this number gets mis-sold. Florida's Rule 62-308.100 contains no compaction percentage, no base depth, and no drainage floor. The enumerated installation numbers that circulated while the rule was being written were proposals in comment letters; they were not adopted. No university standard fills the gap either — the published university guidance on synthetic turf is qualitative and sets no percentage.

So when AJ Turf says 95% Standard Proctor, we are stating our adopted written spec, aligned with the Synthetic Turf Council — not claiming Florida requires it. Any installer who tells you a compaction percentage is "the Florida standard" is selling you a number with no standard behind it.

Florida note

Southwest Florida builds on sand over limestone with a high water table, from Fort Myers and Cape Coral to Naples, Estero, Bonita Springs, Sanibel, and Lehigh Acres. Sand migrates and settles if it is not locked in lifts, and summer storms test every weak spot in the first season. AJ Turf builds around Florida's statewide synthetic turf standard (Rule 62-308.100) — positive drainage, a pervious system, and native soil left able to percolate — and holds its own base to the Synthetic Turf Council's 95% Standard Proctor benchmark on top of that.

The honest downside A written compaction spec is a standard to build to, not a guarantee you can watch being met — a homeowner cannot verify a Proctor percentage by eye on install day, and honest installers will tell you field verification is the firm, non-yielding finish, not a lab test on every backyard. Compaction also has a real ceiling: push past stability in pursuit of hardness and you start closing the pore space the drainage depends on. The spec is a balance struck deliberately, not "harder is always better."

The gotcha The bait is a compaction number quoted with no standard behind it — a percentage an installer cannot tie to a test method or a publishing body, offered because a number sounds more professional than a practice. Ask one question: "Ninety-five percent of what, tested how, published by whom?" AJ Turf's answer fits in one line: 95% Standard Proctor, ASTM D698, Synthetic Turf Council guidance. If the answer you get is a shrug in a lab coat, you are being sold the sound of a standard, not a standard.

How AJ Turf handles this

Every AJ Turf base is built the same way: dig about 2–3 inches below the sod line — never building the base on topsoil — then screening sand, about 1 ton per 100 square feet finished with a minimum 2-inch compacted layer, wetted and compacted in lifts to 95% Standard Proctor (ASTM D698), aligned with and citable to the Synthetic Turf Council. Crushed gravel appears only where feet of fill depth are needed, and it is always capped with screening sand. On top of the compacted base goes the woven weed barrier, then turf glued to a 3×3×3-inch concrete border, with seams on tape and glue underneath. Nails and spikes secure only the barrier and seam tape under the turf — never through the turf itself. Pile direction is set deliberately: backyard installs lean toward the house, front-yard installs toward the street. Sprinkler heads are capped and left in place, never ripped out.

Questions people ask.

Is 95% compaction a Florida requirement?

No. Florida's Rule 62-308.100 sets no compaction percentage at all — its only compaction language limits over-compacting the native soil so percolation is not harmed. The 95% Standard Proctor figure is AJ Turf's adopted written spec, taken from the Synthetic Turf Council's published guidelines for synthetic turf base materials.

How do I know my base was compacted enough if I can't test it?

Use the field version of the spec: properly wetted sand, compacted in lifts, finished firm and non-yielding — it does not move underfoot, and it does not take a footprint. Then watch the first season: settling dips, new low spots, and ripples are the signatures of a base that was not there.

Can a base be compacted too much?

Yes — compaction and permeability pull against each other. Over-compacting closes pore space and slows the water the system needs to move, and Florida's rule expressly protects the percolation of the soil beneath the subgrade. The target is stability at spec, not maximum possible hardness.

Does base compaction affect my turf warranty?

It can. The manufacturer warranty excludes wear from an inadequate sub-base and excludes faulty installation. A soft, settling base is both an install failure and a potential hole in a product claim — which is why the base is quoted and built as a spec, not a judgment call.
