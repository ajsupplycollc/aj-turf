How do I compare two turf quotes spec-for-spec?

By Jereme Strange, AJ Turf · years on the manufacturing side of the turf industry, and years installing and repairing turf in Florida

The short answer

Compare quotes spec-for-spec by lining up the same three things on every bid: face weight — not just total weight — backing type, and drainage rate, then the base, edge, infill, and warranty behind them. If a quote won't name those in writing, it isn't cheaper — it's just hiding where it cut.

The short version — the comparison worksheet

Fill one row per quote. Any cell a quote leaves blank is a finding, not a footnote.

Start with the three questions

When a homeowner waves a competitor quote, the comparison that cuts through it is three questions: face weight, backing type, drainage rate. Cheap quotes almost always lose on at least two of the three — because those are precisely the lines where money can be removed without changing how the quote looks on install day.

Face weight first. Every product has a face weight (the yarn) and a total weight (yarn + backing + coatings). The industry bait is quoting only total, because a heavy backing lets a sparse turf weigh like a dense one. AJ Turf's rule: both numbers, always, with face weight as the comparator. Mainstream landscape turf clusters around 47–72 oz face weight; entry products sit near 25 oz. Full lesson on our face-weight page, linked below.

Backing type second. Ask what the backing is and how water moves through it — punched holes in a coated backing, or a permeable construction that drains across the whole surface. In a dog yard this is half the odor battle: urine that can't drain pools in the base, and no infill fixes architecture.

Drainage rate third. Get the product's drainage rate as quoted, with the condition it was measured under. Then remember the installer's half of drainage: the backing drains vertically, but the base moves water away. A fast backing over a flat, fine-clogged base still puddles — which is why the base row of the worksheet exists.

Then compare the build, not the brochure

Base. The AJ Method benchmark, so you have one concrete build to compare against: dig about 2–3 inches below the sod line, never build on topsoil; a screening-sand base at about 1 ton per 100 sq ft finished, a minimum 2-inch compacted layer, compacted to 95% Standard Proctor (ASTM D698) per Synthetic Turf Council guidance; crushed gravel only where feet of fill depth are needed, always capped with screening sand. Against that, a quote that says "base included" with no material, depth, or compaction has told you nothing — and a sand-only base with no compaction spec is the shortcut behind a season-two lumpy yard. Ask.

Edges. Two builds exist: spikes driven through the turf (point loads that work loose under thermal cycling until edges lift and panels creep), or a continuous perimeter — in the AJ Method, a 3×3×3-inch poured concrete border with the turf glued to it, using turf glue with a 15-year warranty, and nails or spikes only underneath, securing the weed barrier and seam tape, never through the turf. Put the edge method in the worksheet in the installer's own words.

Seams. Joined on seam tape with a full spread of glue, weighted for the cure — or strip-glued and hand-pressed. Seam failure is the most common first-year complaint in this business, and its causes (glue on wet backing, no cure pressure, starved adhesive) are all quote-invisible unless you ask how seams are built.

Infill. Named products, named rates. The baseline is 1.5–2 lb/sq ft. A pet quote should show its odor layer by name and rate — for example, ZeoFill, a zeolite mineral (97% pure clinoptilolite), at 1.5 lb/sq ft for a dog up to 45 lb, 2 lb at 50–65 lb, 3–4 lb for multiple or large dogs — or an antimicrobial coated sand such as Envirofill by Motz at 1.5–2 lb/sq ft. A cooling layer, if quoted, is its own line: TCool at ½–1 lb/sq ft, never mixed with zeolite. One blended line called "infill" is where pet systems quietly become plain sand.

Barrier and the small lines. A woven weed barrier on top of the compacted base, under the turf — woven, not pressed nonwoven. Sprinklers capped and left in place, never ripped out. Pile direction planned: backyard installs lean toward the house, front yards toward the street. Each is a small line; together they're the difference between a method and a crew improvising.

Then compare the paper behind the price

Warranty, exactly. Turf carries the manufacturer's warranty for the product you choose (8, 16, or 25 years depending on the product), covering UV stability and strength only, prorated, non-transferable — plus the installer's own labor warranty, which at AJ Turf is 1 year. Any quote implying the manufacturer covers labor is inventing a term: there is no manufacturer labor coverage in the AJ Turf story, ever. And one warranty fact reframes the whole worksheet: faulty installation and an inadequate sub-base are manufacturer warranty exclusions — so the cheap base doesn't just risk the yard, it risks the claim.

Price basis and staging. An exact price comes from an in-person measurement — one exact written price. Most installs run $9 to $15 a square foot depending on the turf and the site; a quote far below that band, written without seeing the yard, is telling you which worksheet rows it emptied. Staging at AJ Turf is 30% to schedule / 35% the first day on site / 35% on completion, deposits always non-refundable — compare that shape against any quote asking for most of the money up front.

Scope lines. Minimums and timing belong on the worksheet too: AJ Turf installs start at 1,000 sq ft, repairs are assessed free onsite with no minimum, and installs are typically scheduled within two weeks. And if an HOA is involved, one line decides weeks of your life: AJ Turf prepares and submits the HOA application on the homeowner's behalf — ask whether the other quote does, or hands you a packet.

Florida note

In Southwest Florida, weight the drainage and base rows heaviest. Summer storms test the system weekly, canal and flat lots give water nowhere obvious to go, and a base that puddles 30 minutes after rain is feeding the bacteria behind pet odor no surface treatment can out-clean. Florida's Rule 62-308.100 also makes two worksheet rows legal questions now — infill material (clean silica sand, rock, shell, or other natural material) and a washed, pervious subgrade — so a Florida quote that can't speak to the rule is behind the law as well as the craft.

The honest downside A spec-for-spec comparison takes real time, and it has a limit: it compares what's written, and a dishonest installer can write a premium spec and install a lighter product — the density bait-and-switch is invisible to your eyes on install day. That's why the worksheet ends at the truck, not the signature: verify the product that arrives against the product on the quote, both weight numbers. Specs also aren't the whole job — communication, follow-through, and whether the company answers in year two don't fit in a table, and post-install follow-through is repeatedly what separates four stars from five in this market.

The gotcha The cheapest quote is rarely cheaper — it's shorter. It wins by emptying the rows you can't see from the street: face weight swapped for a total-weight headline, an unnamed base, spikes instead of a border, plain sand wearing a "pet turf" label, a warranty with no terms. The worksheet's power is that it makes the cuts visible before they're buried under your lawn. If an installer resists filling it in, the resistance is the answer.

Questions people ask.

Two quotes list the same total weight but very different prices. What explains it?

Start at face weight: total weight can match while the yarn — the part you walk on — differs by 20 ounces, because backing weight fills the gap. Then read the base, edge, and infill rows. Price differences that survive a full worksheet are real scope differences; price differences that vanish were spec differences in disguise.

What if a quote won't give me a face weight or a product name?

Treat the blank as the answer. A quote with no exact product named is one of the classic red flags: it leaves the installer free to install whatever is on the truck. Get the product and both weight numbers in writing before comparing its price to anyone's.

Is the lowest quote ever the right choice?

It can be — if it fills every worksheet row at the same spec: same face weight, same backing class, a real base with a compaction spec, a continuous edge, named infill at named rates, and written warranty terms. Low quotes fail the worksheet far more often than they fail on price alone.

Why does the base belong in a price comparison at all?

Because the manufacturer warranty excludes damage from an inadequate sub-base and faulty installation. A cut-rate base doesn't just risk lumps and washouts — it can void your practical claim path on the turf itself. The base row is a warranty row.
