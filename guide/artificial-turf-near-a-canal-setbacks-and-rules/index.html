I live on a canal — are there setbacks or rules for turf near the water?

By Jereme Strange, AJ Turf · years on the manufacturing side of the turf industry, and years installing and repairing turf in Florida

The short answer

Yes — Florida's statewide turf rule sets a 10-foot setback from any waterbody, measured from the ordinary or mean high water line, unless a physical barrier like a seawall or bulkhead already exists. It's quote-time math: the setback is measured and drawn before any design or price is final.

The short version

The 10-foot rule, plainly

Florida's Rule 62-308.100 — the statewide synthetic turf standard, effective May 19, 2026 — includes a water-quality provision: no installation within 10 feet of any waterbody, measured from the ordinary or mean high water line, unless a physical barrier — a seawall or bulkhead — exists. A canal is a waterbody for this purpose. So is a lake, a pond, or any other water your lot touches.

Read the exception carefully, because it's the sentence waterfront quotes turn on: the exception is about a barrier that already exists between the yard and the water. It is not a promise that building something changes your math, and this page doesn't treat it as one — it's the existing condition of your lot, measured on site, that decides which side of the rule your yard is on.

Why it's quote-time math

The setback isn't paperwork you file after choosing a design — it redraws the design. Ten feet back from the high water line can remove a meaningful strip of a canal yard from the turf area, and that changes the square footage, the layout, the seams, and the price. On a narrower lot it can change whether the install clears the 1,000 sq ft install minimum at all.

That's why canal-front quotes run the setback math before design: measure from the high water line, draw the 10-foot line, and lay the yard out on the landward side of it. A number quoted before that measurement is a number quoted on a yard that may not be the yard you're allowed to build. AJ Turf's installs are built around Florida's statewide synthetic turf standard (Rule 62-308.100) — the 10-foot waterbody setback is named in that approved framing precisely because it's the first measurement on a waterfront quote.

Two more waterfront checks ride along with it:

  • The perimeter check. The rule doesn't address a perimeter element like a concrete border directly — but on a waterfront lot, the border is part of the installation, so the build has to be confirmed as not starting inside the 10-foot setback. It's a layout question, answered on the drawing, not assumed.
  • The runoff check. The same rule requires no pooling and no increased stormwater runoff to adjacent properties, and turf must not alter a permitted stormwater system — on a canal lot, where the yard's water story ends at the water, grading is a compliance question as well as a drainage one. Turf also never goes in a swale, ditch, stormwater pond, or littoral zone, wherever those sit on the lot.

What else the statewide rule means on a waterfront lot

The setback is the headline, not the whole rule. The same standard that sets it also covers the rest of a residential install — permeable turf on permeable backing over a pervious subgrade, positive drainage, washed subgrade materials, no in-ground irrigation used to irrigate the turf area, and septic-tank access preserved where it applies. The rule covers synthetic turf on single-family residential properties of 1 acre or less, and once it took effect, local governments may not ban compliant turf or regulate it inconsistently with the standard.

One boundary, stated plainly because waterfront homeowners ask: AJ Turf does not build, repair, or sell seawalls. A seawall is a different trade with its own licensing. On this page — and in our work — a seawall appears only as the rule does: an existing physical barrier that affects your setback math. If your lot's barrier needs work, that's a conversation with a seawall contractor, before or alongside the turf conversation, not instead of it.

Florida note

Canal-front living is a Southwest Florida standard, not a rarity — Cape Coral's canal system alone means thousands of yards where the back edge of the lot is a waterline, and Fort Myers and Naples add lakes and waterfront lots on top of that. It's also why this content exists here and not on a national turf site: the 10-foot setback, the high-water-line measurement, and the barrier exception are Florida rule, applied to Florida lots, at Florida quotes. Seasonal owners should note the practical side too: the setback strip stays natural — it's the part of the yard nearest the water, and it remains whatever it is today (grass, planting, or hardscape) regardless of what the rest of the yard becomes.

The honest downside The setback can shrink your project in ways a satellite photo won't warn you about. Ten feet off the waterline, on a lot that was already tight, can cut the turf area hard, change the layout you pictured, or in the narrowest cases take a full install below the size where a turf project makes sense as drawn. The exception helps only if a qualifying barrier already exists on your lot — if it doesn't, the 10 feet is the 10 feet, and no installer can design around a state rule for you. Waterfront quotes take a little longer for exactly this reason: the measuring is the work.

The gotcha The waterfront gotcha is the quote written from a satellite image or a drive-by, with the full back yard drawn as turf right down to the water. That quote prices a yard that can't legally be built as drawn — and the discovery gets made at install time, with a deposit already down, as a "site condition." The question that prevents it is one sentence, asked before you sign anything: "Show me the 10-foot line on my lot." If the installer can walk it, measure it, and draw your layout against it, the quote is real. If the setback has never come up on a canal lot, it wasn't measured — it was missed.

Questions people ask.

My seawall is already there. Does the 10-foot setback still apply?

The rule's exception is written for exactly that condition: the 10-foot setback applies unless a physical barrier — a seawall or bulkhead — exists. An existing barrier on your lot is measured and noted at the quote, and the layout is drawn against your lot's actual condition. What the exception doesn't do is promise anything about a barrier that doesn't exist yet.

Is a canal treated the same as a lake or the river?

For this rule, yes — the setback runs from "any waterbody," measured from the ordinary or mean high water line. Canal, lake, pond, or river frontage all start the same measurement. What differs lot to lot is the barrier exception and where the high water line actually falls, which is why it's measured on site.

Can I turf right down to my dock or seawall cap?

Only if your lot's condition puts you inside the rule — the existing-barrier exception — and the layout, perimeter included, is drawn that way at the quote. Where no barrier exists, the turf stops 10 feet back from the high water line, full stop. Nobody should be quoting you turf to the water's edge on an open canal lot.

Does the setback strip have to stay grass?

The rule says what can't go there: turf. It doesn't assign the strip a replacement — it stays whatever it is today, or becomes planting or hardscape by your choice, subject to the same rule's other provisions (turf itself never goes in a swale, ditch, stormwater pond, or littoral zone, and a permitted stormwater system can't be altered). At the quote we'll show you exactly where the line leaves your yard.
