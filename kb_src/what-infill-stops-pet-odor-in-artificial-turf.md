# What infill stops pet odor — and how does it work?

**By Jereme Strange, AJ Turf** — years on the manufacturing side of the turf industry, and years installing and repairing turf in Florida.

**The answer:** ZeoFill — a zeolite mineral that is 97% clinoptilolite — stops pet odor at the source. Its negatively charged honeycomb structure traps ammonium from urine before bacteria convert it to ammonia gas. Rain rinses and recharges it. Antimicrobial coated sand is the other legitimate option, but it works indirectly, by keeping urine from soaking in.

## Why pet turf smells in the first place

Dog urine doesn't stay urine. It drains through the turf and collects in the infill, the backing, and the base underneath. In each of those layers, bacteria break the urea in the urine down into ammonia gas — and ammonia is the smell. Heat speeds that chemistry up, which is why a yard can smell acceptable in the morning and knock you back by mid-afternoon.

That sequence matters because it tells you where an infill has to work. A product that perfumes the surface, or one that only kills bacteria on contact, is acting *after* the ammonia already exists. The infill that actually stops pet odor acts one step earlier in the chain.

## How zeolite stops odor at the source

Zeolite is a natural volcanic mineral with a negatively charged, honeycomb-like internal structure. Urine carries ammonium — the compound bacteria would otherwise convert into ammonia gas. The mineral's charged structure attracts and traps that ammonium *before* the conversion happens. No conversion, no ammonia, no smell at the source.

Two practical consequences follow:

- **It has a capacity.** ZeoFill holds roughly 55% of its own weight in urine, which is why the application rate scales with the size and number of dogs (see the table below). A token sprinkle is not a pet system.
- **It recharges.** Rain rinses the trapped ammonium through and refreshes the mineral — and enzyme treatments do the same job on demand. In heavy use, expect a refresh cycle of roughly 12–24 months: an enzyme recharge or a partial top-up of the odor layer in the dog's habitual spot.

Zeolite is applied as the odor layer over the regular ballast infill, not instead of it. The ballast still does its structural jobs — holding the turf down and supporting the blades — while the zeolite layer handles the chemistry.

## The other legitimate option: antimicrobial coated sand

**Envirofill** by Motz works by a completely different mechanism. It is an acrylic-coated rounded quartz sand with antimicrobial protection infused in the coating. Urine doesn't soak into it — it sits on the coated surface and rinses through, so the infill itself never becomes the odor reservoir, and the antimicrobial coating blocks the bacteria, mold, and mildew growth that drive the smell.

Be clear on the difference: coated sand's odor control is *indirect* — drainage plus antimicrobial action — not capture. It doesn't trap ammonium the way zeolite does. It's a durable, install-and-forget option (the infill itself carries a 16-year warranty), and for many pet yards it's the right fit. But if odor kill at the source is the priority, zeolite is the mechanism built for it.

Plain silica sand, for contrast, has no odor mechanism at all. In a pet area it holds urine against the backing — that is the documented recipe for a yard that smells by month three. Silica's correct role in a pet yard is the ballast layer *under* an odor-active top layer.

## No infill rescues a bad system

Infill is one leg of a three-legged stool. A pet yard that stays clean needs:

1. A **permeable, high-flow backing**, so urine rinses through instead of pooling in the turf.
2. An **odor-active infill layer** — zeolite or antimicrobial coated sand — over the ballast.
3. A **free-draining base**, so what rinses through actually leaves.

Any one leg missing, and the yard smells by August — no matter what the bag said.

## Florida note

Southwest Florida is the hardest test in the country for pet turf: heat and humidity accelerate the bacteria that make ammonia, all year long. The upside is that Florida rain is frequent, and rain is zeolite's recharge mechanism — the climate that makes the odor problem worse also keeps the at-source solution working. What Florida doesn't forgive is a saturated base: if odor survives thorough cleaning and is strongest at ground level, the reservoir is under the turf, and no infill change on top will fix it.

> **The honest downside**
> Zeolite is not install-and-forget forever. It has a finite capacity — roughly 55% of its weight in urine — so a small run used hard by several large dogs will saturate and need an enzyme recharge or partial replacement, typically somewhere in the 12–24 month range in heavy use. The rate, and therefore the cost, also scales steeply with dog load: 3–4 lb per square foot for multiple or large dogs is a lot of mineral. And no infill, zeolite included, fixes a yard whose base is already saturated or whose backing never drained — that's a repair diagnosis, not an infill purchase.

> **The gotcha**
> The most common pet-turf bait is a quote that says "pet turf" and specs plain silica sand. Silica is ballast — it has no odor mechanism, and in a dog yard it holds urine against the backing. By month three the yard smells, and the homeowner gets sold a deodorizer service for a problem that was built in on install day. Ask any installer one question: "What in this quote actually stops odor, and how?" If the answer is a spray, a scent, or just the word "pet" on the turf box, there is no odor system in the quote.

## FAQ

**Does zeolite stop pet odor permanently?**
It stops odor at the source for as long as it has capacity left. ZeoFill holds roughly 55% of its weight in urine; rain rinses and recharges it, and enzyme treatments recharge it on demand. In heavy use, plan on a refresh — an enzyme recharge or a partial top-up — roughly every 12–24 months.

**What's the difference between ZeoFill and Envirofill for odor?**
Mechanism. ZeoFill is zeolite: it captures ammonium before bacteria convert it to ammonia gas. Envirofill by Motz is an antimicrobial coated sand: urine doesn't soak in, it rinses through, and the coating blocks the bacterial growth that causes smell. Capture versus rinse-through — both legitimate, spec'd for different yards.

**Can I just add zeolite on top of my existing turf?**
Sometimes. If the smell lives in the infill, power-brooming out the contaminated infill and replacing it with zeolite at the right rate can fix it. If the smell is strongest at ground level and survives thorough cleaning, the base underneath is saturated — and no top layer will fix that. That diagnosis is what a free onsite assessment is for.

**Why not use a cooling infill for odor too?**
Because cooling and odor are different jobs. TCool cools by evaporation while it's hydrated; it has no odor mechanism, and vendor guidance is explicit that it should never be mixed with zeolite — blending dilutes both. We spec cooling as its own separate layer and option, never as the pet-odor answer.

**How much zeolite does my yard actually need?**
By dog load: 1.5 lb per square foot for a dog up to 45 lb, 2 lb per square foot for a 50–65 lb dog, and 3–4 lb per square foot for multiple or large dogs — as the odor layer over the regular ballast infill. Undersizing it is the most common reason a "zeolite install" disappoints.
