# Why is my turf wrinkling, rippling, or lifting at the edges?

**By Jereme Strange, AJ Turf** — years on the manufacturing side of the turf industry, and years installing and repairing turf in Florida.

**The answer:** Wrinkling, rippling, and lifting edges usually trace to movement underneath or at the perimeter: a loose, spike-secured edge that crept under thermal cycling, too little infill to hold the panel, or a base that was thin or poorly compacted. The fix is re-stretching, re-securing the perimeter properly, and restoring infill — not just brushing.

## Start with the pattern, not the wrinkle

A wrinkle is a symptom. The same-looking ripple can come from the perimeter, the infill, the base, or the turf's own memory — and each one has a different fix at a different price. Before anyone quotes you anything, run the field checks below. They're the same ones we run on a repair call.

| What you see | Likely cause | Field check | The real fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wrinkles/ripples that come and go with the time of day — worse at midday | Thermal expansion against a loose perimeter; spike-secured edges crept | Check the edges for movement; does the wrinkle grow as the yard heats up? | Re-stretch, re-secure the perimeter properly, top infill up to spec |
| Edges lifting at borders or planters | Spikes pulled out; no real edge restraint; water undermining the edge | Lift the edge and inspect the fasteners and the base for wash-out | Rebuild the edge — glued concrete border method; fix the water source |
| The whole panel has shifted off its lines | No perimeter restraint + thermal cycling | Compare the panel's position against patios, borders, and seams | Re-set the panel and glue the perimeter |
| Straight creases at regular intervals, there since install | Turf stored folded or installed without acclimation | Crease lines repeat at even spacing | Steam, stretch, and infill; replace the section if the fiber memory has set |
| Lumps and ripples that don't move with temperature | Base or stretch problems underneath | Walk it: does the surface feel uneven underfoot in the same places? | Lift, rebuild/regrade the base section, re-lay |
| Turf pulling away from a hard edge | The glued perimeter bond has failed or was never continuous | Check whether the separation runs along the whole edge or one spot | Re-bond the perimeter along its full length |

## Cause 1: the perimeter let go

This is the most common one, and it's structural. Turf expands in heat and contracts as it cools, daily. If the panel is held only by spikes, every cycle works those spikes a little looser in Florida sand — each spike is a point load, not a restraint system — until the perimeter creeps. Once the edge can move, the expanding panel has nowhere to go but up: wrinkles and ripples in the field of the yard, lifting at the borders.

The edge itself tells the story. If you can lift the perimeter with your fingers, find pulled or wobbling spikes, or see the panel has shifted off its original lines, the restraint failed — not the turf. Brushing and infill will make it look better for a week. The fix is mechanical: re-stretch the panel and re-secure the perimeter along its whole length. Our method rebuilds the edge as a poured concrete border with the turf glued to it, which distributes the stress along the entire border instead of at spike points.

## Cause 2: not enough infill

Infill is structural, not decorative. It ballasts the panel, and it gives the fibers the support to stand up straight — a finished install should carry at least 1.5 lb per square foot, within the standard 1.5–2 lb per square foot baseline. Under-infilled turf is under-ballasted turf: it moves more with temperature, wrinkles more easily, and mats in the traffic lanes. Infill also migrates and settles over time — check the level with the stiff-brush feel test, compare the traffic lanes against the edges, and top up where it's low. If your wrinkles improve noticeably after a proper top-up and a brush against the grain, infill was at least part of your diagnosis.

## Cause 3: the base underneath

Lumps and ripples that *don't* follow the temperature are reporting from below. A base that was thin, poorly compacted, or built over topsoil settles unevenly; washout steals material from one spot and the surface prints it. You can feel this one underfoot — the unevenness is in the ground, not the grass. No surface treatment fixes a base: the turf comes up in that section, the base gets rebuilt and re-compacted, and the turf goes back down. That's also the moment to check the perimeter and infill while everything is open.

## Grade any finished install — including ours

This is the checklist we judge a finished install by, and it's the fastest way to grade the yard in front of you, whoever built it:

1. **Seams separating** — seams should be closed and invisible, not opening or showing tape lines.
2. **Lumps or ripples** — the surface should lie flat; lumps and ripples mean base or stretch problems underneath.
3. **Turf separating from the border** — a glued perimeter should be bonded along its whole length; any edge pulling away is a fail.
4. **Infill spread evenly** — no white spots where sand gathered in one place, and no dead spots the infill never reached; both are visible from standing height.
5. **Fibers brushed up and standing** — the finished surface should stand, not lie matted.
6. **Adequate infill — at least 1.5 lb per square foot** — because infill is what gives the fibers the structure to stand; items 4 and 5 fail without it.

If your yard fails items 1–3, you're not looking at a maintenance task. You're looking at an installation repair.

## Florida note

Florida runs this failure on fast-forward. Daily thermal cycling is relentless, summer rain saturates the sand that loose spikes are sitting in, and a perimeter that would creep over five years somewhere mild lets go here in a summer or two. The storm version of the same logic: edges are the failure point in high wind, and an edge glued to concrete along its full length holds where spikes in saturated sand pull. If your wrinkles appeared or worsened after your first full Florida summer, that's not bad luck — that's the climate reading the install.

> **The honest downside**
> Sometimes the honest diagnosis is bigger than the symptom. A wrinkle caused by a failed base means lifting turf and rebuilding underneath it, not a service visit with a power broom. A crease from folded storage may never fully release if the fiber memory has set — steam and stretch help, but replacement is sometimes the truth. And re-spiking a perimeter that already worked loose is money spent twice: the same point loads in the same sand will creep again. We'll tell you which size of problem you actually have before we quote the fix.

> **The gotcha**
> The cheap fix pitch for wrinkles is "it just needs stretching and more sand" — quoted over the phone, without anyone lifting an edge. Sometimes that's even true. But if the perimeter restraint failed or the base moved, stretching a panel tight against a loose edge just stores up the next ripple, and you'll pay for the same wrinkle twice. Ask anyone quoting a wrinkle fix one question: "What let the panel move — the edge, the infill, or the base — and how did you check?" If nobody lifted an edge or checked infill depth, nobody diagnosed anything.

## FAQ

**Why are my turf wrinkles worse in the middle of the day?**
Heat. Turf expands as it warms, and a panel whose perimeter has crept loose has nowhere for that expansion to go but up into ripples. Wrinkles that come and go with temperature point at the perimeter and infill level first — not at the base, and not at a defective product.

**Can I fix lifting edges myself by adding more spikes?**
You can pin an edge back down temporarily, but understand what you're repeating: spikes are point loads in sand, they work loose under the same thermal cycling that freed the originals, and a spike coming up out of turf is a genuine injury risk. The durable fix is a continuous bonded edge — in our method, turf glued to a poured concrete border.

**How do I check whether low infill is my problem?**
Compare the traffic lanes to the edges: if the pile lies flat and feels thin and hard underfoot where people walk, and lusher at the borders, infill has migrated or was low to start with. A finished install should carry at least 1.5 lb per square foot. Topping up to spec and brushing against the grain is the test — if the wrinkles mostly leave, infill was the diagnosis.

**My wrinkles don't change with temperature. What does that mean?**
That pattern points underneath: lumps and ripples that hold still all day mean base or stretch problems — settlement, washout, or a base that was never compacted flat. That repair means lifting the affected section and rebuilding the base, which is exactly what the free onsite assessment is for.

**Will a repair match the rest of my yard?**
Seam, edge, infill, and base repairs work with your existing turf, so there's no new-panel color or direction mismatch to manage. The one check we make first: panels originally laid in mismatched directions can't be blended by a repair — that fix is re-laying the offending panel in the correct direction.
