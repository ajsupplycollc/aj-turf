# What is face weight, and what number should I look for?

**By Jereme Strange, AJ Turf** — years on the manufacturing side of the turf industry, and years installing and repairing turf in Florida.

**The answer:** Face weight is the ounces of yarn per square yard above the backing — the grass you actually walk on. Total weight adds the backing and coatings and is always bigger. Compare face weight first: mainstream landscape turf clusters around 47–72 ounces, with entry products near 25 and premium products higher.

## The short version

| Number | What it counts | Range in real landscape products | How to use it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Face weight** | Yarn above the backing only — the pile itself (ASTM D5848) | ~25 oz entry · **~47–72 oz mainstream landscape cluster** · up to ~84 oz premium | **The honest density comparator.** Compare quotes on this number first |
| **Total weight** | Face weight **plus** primary backing, secondary backing, and coatings | ~53–125 oz | Context only. A heavy backing can make sparse turf "weigh" like dense turf |
| **Pile height** | Blade length above the backing | ~1.35–2″ in the landscape cluster; pet products shorter at ~1–1.5″ | Height is not density — a tall, sparse lawn is still sparse |
| **Machine gauge** | Spacing between stitch rows | 3/8″ is nearly universal in landscape turf | Part of the density picture alongside face weight and stitch count |

## Face weight vs. total weight: what's the difference?

Every turf product carries **both** numbers, because a turf product is two things stacked: yarn on top, and the backing system underneath that locks the yarn in. **Face weight** weighs only the yarn — the fibers standing above the backing. **Total weight** weighs everything: yarn, primary backing, secondary backing, and the coating that binds them.

Total weight is always the bigger number, and that is exactly the problem. A product can gain total weight in the backing — thicker coating, heavier secondary layer — while the lawn you see and walk on stays thin. Two products can show the same total weight and be completely different lawns. They cannot show the same face weight and hide it: face weight is a direct measure of how much grass is in the grass.

That is why AJ Turf's publishing rule is simple: **quote both numbers, always.** Face weight is what the buyer is actually paying for; total weight is published alongside it so nothing is hidden in either direction.

## What number should you actually look for?

There is no single magic ounce — anyone who gives you one threshold for every yard is selling, not teaching. What the real product range tells you is where a quote sits:

- **Near ~25 oz face weight** is entry-level territory. It has a place in light-use areas, but in a trafficked Florida yard, low face weight is the product that mats within a few seasons — density is resilience.
- **The mainstream landscape cluster runs ~47–72 oz.** Most honest residential quotes live here, and the right point inside the cluster depends on traffic: kids, dogs, and daily paths argue for the denser end; a decorative front lawn argues less.
- **Premium landscape products run higher, up to ~84 oz** in the spec sets we work from — more yarn, more resilience, more money.

Then check it with your eyes, using the field tell: **sparse turf shows black backing between the stitch rows.** Dense turf hides its backing under yarn. If you can see rows of black between the blades on a sample, the face weight is telling the same story the sample is — believe both.

## Why does density matter more in Florida?

Density is what stands up to use. A blade field with enough yarn supports itself, springs back after foot traffic, and hides its backing from the sun and from view. A sparse field folds, mats in the traffic lanes, and shows its rows. In Southwest Florida that plays out fast: year-round growth-season use, daily dog traffic, furniture and play equipment that never come in for a winter. The repair-bench version of a low-face-weight purchase is matting and flat traffic lanes a few seasons in — and matting caused by spent, sparse yarn is a product-life problem, not a cleaning problem. Power-brooming and fresh infill help a lawn that has density left; they can't add yarn that was never there.

Face weight also interacts with the rest of the spec, which is why it leads a three-question comparison rather than standing alone: **face weight, backing type, and drainage rate.** A dense lawn on a backing that drains poorly, in a dog yard, still fails — just more expensively. ## What else belongs on the spec sheet?

Face weight is the headline, not the whole document. A complete quote also lets you verify:

- **Yarn denier** — the thickness/weight of the fiber itself; a real, measurable spec, not an adjective.
- **Stitch count and gauge** — how tightly the yarn is tufted; 3/8″ gauge is the landscape standard.
- **Tuft bind** (ASTM D1335) — the force required to pull a tuft out of the backing; the number behind shedding blades and seam stress.
- **Backing type, named plainly** — polyurethane backings are tough, tear-resistant, and don't crumble in harsh weather; the backing is also where drainage is decided, from punched holes to fully permeable flow-through constructions.
- **Pile height matched to use** — pet products run shorter (~1–1.5″) because shorter pile is easier to clean and carries lower odor and bacteria risk.

If a quote gives you none of this and only a product nickname and a price, you don't have a quote yet. You have an advertisement.

## Florida note

Florida exposes a thin lawn faster than a mild climate does: intense UV, year-round traffic, and summer storms that test drainage weekly. That's the practical reason the face-weight conversation belongs at the front of a Southwest Florida purchase, and why the sample check matters — hold the actual product, look down into the pile, and look for black backing showing between the rows before the truck ever reaches your yard.

> **The honest downside**
> Face weight is the honest comparator, but it is not a complete quality score. A heavy lawn can still have weak tuft bind, a backing wrong for a dog yard, or yarn that mats because of its shape — density is one axis, not the product. Denser turf also costs more, and past the traffic your yard actually sees, extra ounces buy diminishing returns. And the number is only as good as the product that arrives: a quote can print a premium face weight and install a lighter one, invisible to your eyes on install day. Verify the product on the truck against the quote — both numbers, not one.

> **The gotcha**
> The industry bait is quoting **only total weight**, because it's the bigger number and it "seems better." The market shifted to total-weight quoting for exactly that reason — a heavy backing lets a sparse turf weigh like a dense one. So the gotcha has a one-line antidote: **any quote that shows one weight number is hiding the comparison.** Ask for the face weight in writing. If it isn't on the quote, it isn't an accident.

## FAQ

**Is higher face weight always better?**
No — it's better up to the traffic your yard actually sees. Density buys resilience in traffic lanes, around doors, and in dog areas. In a light-use decorative area, a mainstream face weight serves as well as a premium one. What never serves is a low face weight sold at a premium price under a total-weight headline.

**A quote lists an 80-ounce turf. Is that a dense lawn?**
You can't tell until you know which 80 ounces. If it's total weight, the face weight could be far lower — the backing and coatings make up the rest. Ask for the face weight in writing and compare that number against the ~47–72 oz mainstream landscape cluster.

**Can I verify face weight myself?**
On paper, yes: it should be printed on the product's spec sheet as pile yarn weight (ASTM D5848), next to total weight. In person, use the field check — look down into the pile on a sample. Sparse turf shows black backing between the stitch rows; dense turf doesn't.

**Does pile height make up for low face weight?**
No. Height and density are different axes. A tall, sparse lawn still mats, still shows its backing, and holds more heat and debris than a shorter, denser one. Match pile height to the use — shorter for pets, plusher for a showcase lawn — and judge density by face weight.
