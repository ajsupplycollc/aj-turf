# What is thatch in artificial turf, and why does it matter?

**By Jereme Strange, AJ Turf** — years on the manufacturing side of the turf industry, and years installing and repairing turf in Florida.

**The answer:** Thatch is a second, shorter yarn built into the turf at the base of the pile — thin, curly, tan or gold fibers that mimic the dead-grass layer of a real lawn. It adds visual depth and realism, props the taller blades up, hides the backing between stitch rows, helps deflect heat, and reduces how much infill the turf needs.

## What thatch does

| Job | How thatch does it |
|---|---|
| Looks real | Tan/gold tones at the base mimic the dead-grass layer every real Florida lawn shows |
| Supports the blades | The curly secondary yarn braces the primary blades so they stand instead of flopping |
| Hides the backing | Fills the gaps between stitch rows so you don't see black backing through the pile |
| Manages heat and infill | Helps deflect heat at the surface and reduces the infill the product needs |

## Two yarns, one product

Most realistic landscape turf is a dual-yarn construction. The **primary yarn** is the tall blade you walk on and judge by shape and color. The **thatch** is a secondary yarn — shorter, thinner, and texturized so it curls — tufted into the same rows at the base.

In a real lawn, thatch is the layer of living and dead stems and roots between the green blades and the soil. You see it every time you part the grass: brownish, tangled, and slightly springy. Turf thatch copies that layer on purpose. Without it, a synthetic lawn is just green blades rising out of a flat black sheet — and your eye catches the difference even if you can't name it.

## Why it changes how a lawn looks

Realism in turf is mostly decided in the bottom half-inch. Thatch adds the color variation real grass has at its base, breaks up the uniform green, and gives the pile depth when you look down into it. It is also a practical disguise: on a sparse product with low face weight, the stitch rows spread apart and the black backing shows through. Thatch fills that visual gap — which, as the gotcha below explains, cuts both ways.

The support job matters just as much. The curly thatch acts like a spring mattress under the primary blades, helping them recover after foot traffic instead of laying over. It is one of the reasons two products with the same pile height can age very differently in a walkway or a dog run.

## Thatch, infill, and pile height — how they interact

Thatch and infill do overlapping work: both support the blades. A thatched product typically needs less infill to stand up than a thatch-free product, and standard install practice buries the top of the infill slightly *under* the thatch on thatched turf — the thatch should still be visible doing its job, not buried under sand. AJ Turf's standard infill baseline is 1.5–2 lb per square foot, adjusted to the product.

Putting surfaces are the exception that proves the rule: putting-green fiber is short, dense, texturized yarn built for ball roll, not a lawn look — a different construction for a different job, which is why pile height and construction have to be matched to the use (lawns, pets, and greens are three different answers).

## Florida note

In Southwest Florida, realism is tested by the lawns around you: St. Augustine and zoysia yards show a visible tan thatch layer most of the year, especially coming out of the dry season. A turf with no base layer reads as flat and artificial next to them. Thatch also helps deflect heat at the surface — a small contribution, not a cooling system, in a climate where midday surface heat is turf's honest weakness.

> **The honest downside**
> Thatch is not free performance. It is part of why realistic products cost more than flat, thatch-free budget turf. In pet areas, the dense base layer gives urine and debris one more place to linger, which is why pet yards still need regular rinsing and the right infill system — thatch doesn't do odor control. And thatch can't rescue a sparse product forever: it hides the backing visually, but it doesn't add the blade density that keeps a high-traffic yard from matting.

> **The gotcha**
> Thatch is also a camouflage trick. A low-face-weight product with heavy thatch can look full in a showroom sample while the actual grass — the yarn you walk on — is thin. When you compare samples, part the pile and look at what's underneath: how much of what you see is tall blade, and how much is short curly filler? Then ask for the face weight. Thatch should be the supporting actor, never the whole cast.

## FAQ

**Is thatch the same thing as infill?**
No. Thatch is yarn — it's manufactured into the turf and never gets added or topped up. Infill is the sand or specialty material brushed into the pile after install. They do overlapping support work, which is why thatched turf typically needs less infill.

**Does every artificial turf have thatch?**
No. Many budget landscape products and most putting surfaces are thatch-free — greens use short texturized yarn built for ball roll instead. Thatch is a feature of landscape products built to mimic a real lawn's base layer.

**What color should thatch be?**
Tan, gold, or a muted green-brown — the colors of the dead-grass layer in a real lawn. Bright green thatch defeats the purpose; the contrast at the base is what makes the pile read as depth instead of plastic.

**Does thatch help with matting?**
It helps the blades stand and recover by bracing them at the base, but it doesn't prevent matting on its own. Infill depth, face weight, blade shape, and periodic brushing against the grain all share that job — and a low-density product will still mat in a traffic lane.
