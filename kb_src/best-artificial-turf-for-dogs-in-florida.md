# What's the right artificial turf system for dogs in Florida?

**By Jereme Strange, AJ Turf** — years on the manufacturing side of the turf industry, and years installing and repairing turf in Florida.

**The answer:** A dog yard in Florida needs a system, not just "pet turf": a backing that drains through instead of holding urine, an odor-control infill layer such as zeolite on top of the sand ballast, and a free-draining compacted base. Plain silica sand on its own is the classic failure, because it holds urine against the backing. Miss any one of the three and the yard tends to smell by the first summer.

## The short version

| Part of the system | What to look for | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Turf | Shorter pile, about 1 to 1.5 inches, on a backing that drains | Easier to clean, and urine rinses through instead of pooling |
| Odor layer | Zeolite (ZeoFill) over the sand ballast | Traps the compound in urine before it turns into ammonia |
| Antimicrobial option | A coated sand such as Envirofill | Keeps the infill itself from becoming the reservoir |
| Base | Compacted, graded, free-draining screening sand | Urine and rinse water have to leave, or odor lives in the ground |
| Edges | Glued to a concrete border, no spikes through the turf | Nothing for running dogs to work loose |

## How much zeolite does a dog yard need?

The zeolite layer is sized to the dog. Typical rates for ZeoFill, laid over the silica ballast:

| Dogs | ZeoFill per square foot |
|---|---|
| One dog up to about 45 lb | 1.5 lb |
| One dog about 50 to 65 lb | 2 lb |
| Several dogs, or large dogs | 3 to 4 lb |

Zeolite works by trapping ammonium, the step before ammonia gas, so it stops the smell earlier than anything that only masks it. It can hold roughly half its weight in urine, and rain and enzyme cleaners recharge it. In heavy use it typically needs refreshing every one to two years.

## Why does plain sand fail in a dog run?

Silica is the right ballast: it weights the turf and holds the blades up. For lawns and pet areas the grade sold for this is 30/65 silica, at 1.5 to 2 pounds per square foot. But on its own in a dog area it holds urine against the backing, where bacteria turn it into ammonia. That's why pet systems put silica underneath and an odor-active layer on top.

## What about heat and paws?

Florida sun makes any turf hotter than grass at midday. A quick hose rinse cools it fast, shade helps, and a cooling infill such as TCool helps while it's damp. Cooling infill and zeolite are kept as separate options, never mixed together, because each dilutes the other.

## Florida note

Heat and humidity speed up the bacteria that make ammonia, all year round in Southwest Florida. Drainage matters just as much: if a dog yard still puddles half an hour after rain, water is trapped under the turf, and no amount of surface cleaning keeps up.

> **The honest downside**
> No dog yard is maintenance-free. Solids need picking up, the areas the dogs use need regular rinsing, and the zeolite layer needs refreshing over time. And no infill rescues a bad system: a backing that doesn't drain or a base that holds water will smell no matter what's brushed on top.

> **The gotcha**
> Plenty of "pet turf" quotes are regular turf with plain silica sand. That's missing two of the three legs. Ask what the odor layer is, how much of it goes down for your dogs, and what the backing does with liquid.

## FAQ

**What pile height is best for dogs?**
Shorter is easier: about 1 to 1.5 inches. Waste is easier to pick up and rinse away, and there's less depth for odor and bacteria to hide in.

**How often should I rinse a dog area?**
Regularly, focused on the spots the dogs actually use, and more often in dry spells. Rain does a lot of the work. An enzyme cleaner handles stubborn spots, because it breaks down the uric acid crystals water alone leaves behind.

**Can I add zeolite to turf that's already installed?**
Yes. The existing infill in the dog's spots can be brushed out and replaced with zeolite. If the smell is coming from a saturated base, though, the base has to be dealt with first.

**Will my dog dig it up?**
Turf can't be dug up the way grass can, and a glued border leaves no edges or spikes to pull at. Determined chewers can still damage any surface, so supervise a puppy on new turf.
