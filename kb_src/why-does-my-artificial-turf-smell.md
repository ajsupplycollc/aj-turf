# Why does my artificial turf smell — and how do you actually fix it?

**By Jereme Strange, AJ Turf** — years on the manufacturing side of the turf industry, and years installing and repairing turf in Florida.

**The answer:** Artificial turf smells because dog urine doesn't stay urine: bacteria in the infill, backing, and base break its urea down into ammonia gas, and Florida heat speeds that chemistry up. Sprays only treat the surface. The real fix climbs a ladder — enzyme treatment, fresh infill, a backing-level soak, and, if the base is saturated, replacing the contaminated stone underneath.

## The short version

| Level | The fix | When it's the right level |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Husbandry reset: rinse + enzyme cleaner | Smell is recent and mostly at the surface |
| 2 | Power-broom out contaminated infill, replace with zeolite | Smell lives in the dog's habitual spot |
| 3 | Enzyme soak flooded through to the backing | Cleaning helps for a few days, then it's back |
| 4 | Lift the turf, remove/replace the contaminated base stone | Smell is strongest at ground level and survives everything |
| 5 | Re-install with a proper pet-turf system | The turf itself is budget perforated-backing product that never drained |

## Is the smell in the turf — or under it?

Both are possible, and telling them apart is the whole diagnosis. Urine doesn't sit on top of artificial turf. It drains down and collects in three places: the **infill** between the blades, the **backing** the fibers are locked into, and the **base** underneath everything. Plain silica-sand infill — the cheapest and most common — actually holds urine against the backing instead of letting it rinse through.

In each of those layers, urease-producing bacteria go to work on the urea in the urine and convert it to ammonia gas. That gas is the smell. It's why a yard can smell fine in the morning and knock you back at 2 p.m.: heat accelerates the chemistry, and the gas releases as the yard warms up.

The field test is simple. Get down at ground level in the dog's habitual spot. If the smell is strongest *at the ground* — and it comes back within days of a thorough cleaning — the reservoir isn't the turf anymore. It's the base. Months of urine absorbed into the base material doesn't rinse out, because the base itself is what's contaminated.

## What won't fix it

Hosing helps — water is the solvent, and regular rinsing is part of owning pet turf — but water alone won't dissolve the uric acid crystals that urine leaves behind. That takes an enzyme-based cleaner; the enzymes digest the crystals. Plain disinfectant kills bacteria on contact but does not remove the source, so the smell rebuilds as soon as new bacteria colonize the same crystals. Deodorizer sprays are a mask, not a fix.

Antimicrobial protection built into a turf's backing is real and useful — it's mixed into the backing during manufacturing, doesn't rinse off, and stops bacterial growth *in the turf itself*. But be clear on what it can't do: it does not decontaminate a saturated base underneath an existing install. If the smell lives in the ground, no backing technology reaches it.

## The fix, in order

**Level 1 — Husbandry reset.** Remove solids, hose-flush the area, and treat with an enzyme-based turf cleaner. This is also the standing routine for any pet yard: rinse regularly, more often where the dogs actually go.

**Level 2 — Infill intervention.** Power-broom out the contaminated infill in the dog's spot and replace it with **zeolite**, a natural volcanic mineral whose honeycomb structure traps ammonium *before* bacteria can convert it to ammonia gas — it attacks the chemistry one step earlier than anything else. (Rain helps flush and recharge it.) An antimicrobial-coated infill is the other legitimate option at this level.

**Level 3 — Backing-level treatment.** Flood an enzyme soak through the turf so it reaches the backing/base interface, and repeat over days. This is the level for installs where cleaning works — briefly.

**Level 4 — Base remediation.** On chronic installs, the honest fix is construction: lift the turf, remove and replace the top layer of contaminated aggregate, and re-lay the turf over a clean base with a proper pet stack. This is the step nobody wants to sell you, because it's real work — and it's the step that actually ends the problem.

**Level 5 — Re-install with the pet system.** Some budget turf has a perforated backing that was never going to drain a dog yard properly. No amount of cleaning fixes architecture. When the product itself is the problem, the fix is replacing it with a system built for pets.

A correct pet system, for the record, has three legs: a **permeable, high-flow backing**, **zeolite or antimicrobial-coated infill** over the ballast layer, and a **free-draining base**. Any one leg missing, and the yard smells by August.

## Florida note

Southwest Florida is the worst case in the continental U.S. for this problem: heat and humidity accelerate the bacteria that make ammonia, all year. Drainage matters here for the same reason — if a yard still puddles 30 minutes after rain, water is trapped under the turf feeding bacteria in the base, and no surface treatment will keep up with that. Odor that rinsing won't fix is often a drainage diagnosis wearing a smell costume.

> **The honest downside**
> There is no spray-on permanent fix, and anyone who sells you one is selling you a mask. Enzyme treatments on a saturated install need repeat applications over days, and they still may only buy time. If the base is saturated, the real fix means lifting the turf and replacing contaminated stone — construction, not cleaning, with a construction price tag. And some cheap turf genuinely cannot be saved: if the backing never drained, cleaning it is money spent delaying a re-install. Pet turf also asks something of you forever — regular rinsing where the dogs go. "Zero maintenance" was never true for a dog yard.

> **The gotcha**
> Here's what the spray-bottle pitch leaves out: on most smelly yards we get called to, **the smell isn't in the grass anymore — it's in the ground under it.** A company quoting a deodorizer treatment for a three-year-old dog yard hasn't diagnosed anything. Ask any installer who quotes you an odor fix one question: "Which layer is the smell in — infill, backing, or base — and how did you check?" If they can't answer, they're guessing with your money.

## FAQ

**I hose my turf down and it still smells a day later. Why?**
Because hosing only handles what's on the surface. Uric acid crystals don't dissolve in water — they need an enzyme cleaner to digest them. And if the smell returns fast, the reservoir is probably deeper: urine that has soaked into the base keeps generating ammonia no matter how clean the top is.

**How can I tell if the smell is in the turf or in the ground under it?**
Get down to ground level in the dog's habitual spot. Smell that's strongest at ground level, and that survives thorough cleaning, is the field signature of a saturated base. Smell that's mostly noticeable after the sun hits a freshly used area is more likely infill-level — and fixable at Level 2.

**My turf has antimicrobial backing. Can it still smell?**
Yes. Antimicrobial protection in the backing stops bacterial growth in the turf itself — it never claimed to clean the ground underneath. A yard can have excellent turf over a contaminated base and smell terrible. The two problems need two different fixes.

**What should a pet-turf install include so this doesn't happen?**
Three legs: a permeable, high-flow backing so urine rinses through instead of pooling; zeolite or antimicrobial-coated infill in the dog's area to intercept ammonium before it becomes ammonia; and a free-draining base. A "pet turf" quote that's just regular turf and silica sand is missing two of the three.
