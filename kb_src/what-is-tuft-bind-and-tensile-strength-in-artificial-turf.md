# What is tuft bind / tensile strength — and why does it matter if I have dogs?

**By Jereme Strange, AJ Turf** — years on the manufacturing side of the turf industry, and years installing and repairing turf in Florida.

**The answer:** Tuft bind is the force required to pull a single tuft of yarn out of the turf's backing, measured under ASTM D1335. Over 8 pounds of force is the published benchmark on quality spec sheets. It matters with dogs because paws, claws, digging, and dragging toys all pull at tufts — weak bind sheds blades and lets seams creep.

## The spec numbers

| Spec | What it measures | The benchmark / range |
|---|---|---|
| Tuft bind (ASTM D1335) | Force to pull one tuft out of the backing | **Over 8 lb** is the published benchmark on quality spec sheets |
| Tuft bind, tested products in our lab set | Same test, across a full product line | **3.0–18.2 lb** — the lowest reading was on a pet product, the highest on a natural-look product |
| Tensile / grab strength | Force to tear the turf sheet itself | The sheet-level partner to tuft bind: tuft bind holds the yarn in; tensile strength holds the product together |

## What tuft bind actually is

Every blade you see is a tuft of yarn stitched through a primary backing and then locked in place by the backing coating. Tuft bind measures how hard that lock holds. In the ASTM D1335 test, a tuft is gripped and pulled straight out, and the peak force is recorded in pounds.

That makes tuft bind a backing story as much as a yarn story. A strong yarn in a weak coating still pulls out. This is also why backing type matters in Florida: polyurethane coatings hold tuft bind and dimensional stability better in hot, wet conditions than latex, which absorbs moisture and degrades faster.

## Why dogs test it harder than people do

Feet mostly press turf *down*. Dogs pull at it *sideways and up*:

- **Claws on the turn.** A dog sprinting and cutting digs its claws in and twists — a direct pull on individual tufts.
- **Digging and scratching.** Even brief digging concentrates force on a small patch of yarn and backing.
- **Dragging and tugging.** Toys, sticks, and play pull across the pile, loading tufts and the seams between panels.
- **Seam stress.** Seams are where two panels meet; weak bind near a seam lets the seam creep and open over time.

That is why tuft bind is often called the dog-drag and seam-stress number. A yard can look fine on day one with a low-bind product and be shedding blades and creeping at the seams within a few seasons of real dog use.

One honest wrinkle from the lab data: across the product line we reviewed, the *lowest* tuft-bind reading was on a product sold for pet use, and the highest was on a natural-look landscape product. The label on the brochure is not the test result. Ask for the number.

## Tensile strength, in plain terms

Tensile strength is the sheet-level version of the same idea: how much force the turf itself takes before it tears. Where tuft bind answers "will the grass stay in the backing?", tensile strength answers "will the backing and the sheet hold together under load?" Both matter at seams, at edges, and anywhere the turf is handled, stretched, or stressed during install and use. A product can pass one and disappoint on the other, which is why a serious spec sheet reports tuft bind by name, with its test method.

## Florida note

Florida heat and humidity are hard on backing coatings. A coating that absorbs moisture and softens loses its grip on the yarn over time, so a tuft-bind number measured on a new roll only tells the whole story if the backing system holds up in a hot, wet climate. Polyurethane backings are the Florida-correct default for exactly this reason — better tuft bind retention and no moisture uptake compared with latex.

> **The honest downside**
> Tuft bind is a lab number on a new product. It does not guarantee how a specific yard ages — install quality, infill depth, and how hard your dogs actually dig all move the real-world result. A high-bind product installed badly, with seams glued on wet backing or left unweighted while they cure, can still open at the seams. And no tuft bind makes turf dig-proof: a determined digger can damage any residential product.

> **The gotcha**
> Most quotes never mention tuft bind at all — you get a pile height, a face weight, and a price. A product can photograph beautifully and still test at the bottom of the tuft-bind range. If a seller calls a product their "pet turf," ask one question: "What is its tuft bind under ASTM D1335?" Over 8 lb is the quality benchmark. If they can't answer, or the sheet doesn't carry the test, you're being asked to trust the label instead of the lab.

## FAQ

**What is a good tuft bind number for artificial turf?**
Over 8 lb of force under ASTM D1335 is the published benchmark on quality spec sheets. In the lab set behind our product line, tested products ranged from 3.0 to 18.2 lb — so "good" is a real, checkable gap, not a rounding difference.

**Does tuft bind matter if I don't have dogs?**
Less, but it still matters. Tuft bind is also the seam-stress number: it helps seams stay closed and blades stay put under foot traffic, furniture dragging, and thermal movement. Dogs just test it fastest and hardest.

**Can I check tuft bind on a sample by pulling the grass?**
A hand pull tells you almost nothing — you can't judge 3 lb against 10 lb by feel, and a new sample hasn't aged in Florida heat. Ask for the ASTM D1335 figure from the spec sheet or lab data for the exact product being quoted.

**Does infill affect tuft bind?**
Infill doesn't change the lab number, but it changes how much stress reaches the tufts in use. Infill at the standard 1.5–2 lb per square foot supports the blades and limits the sideways bending and twisting that work tufts loose over time — one reason under-infilled pet yards shed and mat early.
