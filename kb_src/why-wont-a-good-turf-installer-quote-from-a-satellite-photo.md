# Why won't a good installer just quote me from a satellite photo?

**By Jereme Strange, AJ Turf** — years on the manufacturing side of the turf industry, and years installing and repairing turf in Florida.

**The answer:** A satellite photo cannot see access, drainage, existing grade, borders, sprinkler heads, tree roots, or what the base has to replace — and those are what set the real price. A good installer will give a $9–$15 range remotely, then one exact price after measuring the yard in person, free.

## What a satellite photo actually shows

A satellite image shows a green rectangle and its rough dimensions. That's genuinely useful — for a *range*. It's how we can say, honestly and remotely, that most installs run $9–$15 per square foot depending on the turf and the site. What it cannot do is turn that range into an exact price, because an exact price is mostly a price on things the photo doesn't contain.

Think about what an install actually is: excavation, base construction, drainage, borders, seams, and infill — priced off the conditions found in the yard. The photograph prices only the top layer. Everything underneath it is a guess, and guesses get resolved in exactly two ways: the installer pads the quote to cover the unknowns, or the installer quotes the fantasy version and "discovers" the real conditions after you've signed. Neither one is a favor to you.

## The seven things that set the real price — none visible from orbit

| Price driver | Why a photo can't see it | Why it moves the price |
|---|---|---|
| Site conditions under the grass | Grass hides topsoil, roots, old material, and what excavation will actually meet | The base is never built on topsoil; what's there decides the dig |
| Access | A photo shows a yard, not whether material and equipment can reach it, or by what path | Hand-carried yards and open-access yards are different jobs |
| Drainage and grade | Standing water, flat spots, and negative grade don't photograph | The base must be graded for positive drainage; flat Southwest Florida lots often need drainage designed in |
| Existing borders and edges | A photo can't tell what the turf will butt against — or what's missing | Every edge needs a plan; in the AJ Method, a poured concrete border the turf glues to |
| Sprinkler heads and irrigation | Heads are invisible at satellite resolution | Heads in the turf area are capped and left in place — counted and located in person |
| Trees and roots | Canopy shows; drip lines and root zones don't | Florida's rule keeps installs out of tree drip lines unless an arborist certifies no harm |
| Exact measurement | Roofs, shadows, and image scale distort dimensions | Seams are planned around the 15-ft roll width; a few feet of error changes cuts, seams, and material |

Add the yard-specific design questions a photo can't ask: Is any of this within 10 feet of a waterbody, where Florida's setback applies? Where does water currently go in a summer downpour? Which way should the pile lean — backyard toward the house, front yard toward the street — and from which door do you actually view the yard? The answers change the plan, and the plan is the price.

## Why "exact by satellite" quotes resolve badly

Price creep between quote and invoice is one of the top complaint themes in this market — and the satellite quote is its favorite mechanism. The sequence runs like this: a firm number is given from a photo, the deposit is taken, the crew opens the yard and finds the conditions the photo hid, and the price "has to" change. At that point the homeowner's leverage is gone. The alternative version is quieter: the number never changes, and instead the *job* changes — less base, less infill, a faster perimeter — which is how a yard gets built to a photo's assumptions instead of the ground's reality.

The onsite model exists specifically to kill that sequence. One exact written price, measured in person, that both sides can hold: no mid-job discoveries billed as surprises, because the discovering happened before the signature.

## What an onsite quote actually involves

Standing in the yard, the installer measures it properly, checks access, reads the grade and where water goes, locates sprinkler heads, looks at edges and borders, checks trees and any waterbody setback, and walks the reflection risk — low-E windows that can melt a stripe in turf are far easier to flag in person than from a satellite. That's also when the real product conversation happens: which turf, at what face weight and total weight, with samples in your hand and your light — and, for dog yards, which infill layer. The visit is free, and the output is a single exact price, staged 30% to schedule, 35% at install start, 35% on completion (always non-refundable), with the warranty story in writing: the manufacturer's warranty for the product you choose — 8, 16, or 25 years by product, covering UV stability and strength only, prorated and non-transferable — plus AJ Turf's 1-year labor warranty. If your yard is in an HOA, the visit is also when that gets planned; we prepare and submit the HOA application on your behalf.

## Florida note

Southwest Florida yards are especially badly served by satellite quoting. The region's defining variables are all subsurface or seasonal: a high water table, sand that migrates and won't hold a spike perimeter, canal lots where the 10-foot waterbody setback in Florida's statewide synthetic turf standard (Rule 62-308.100) can reshape the design, and grades so flat that drainage has to be engineered rather than assumed. A photograph taken on a dry winter morning contains none of that. Our installs are built around Florida's statewide synthetic turf standard (Rule 62-308.100) — and you can't verify a single one of its site conditions from orbit.

> **The honest downside**
> The onsite model costs you one thing: a visit. A satellite quote is faster and feels easier, and if all you want today is a ballpark, the $9–$15 per square foot range is the honest remote answer — we'll give it to you on a call without any pressure to book. What we won't do is dress that range up as an exact price. And to be fair to the other side of the trade: an in-person quote is also the moment a bad installer can pressure you. The protection isn't the format — it's leaving the visit with one exact written price, the product and method named, and no obligation beyond it.

> **The gotcha**
> The satellite quote is rarely a convenience — it's a funnel. The low, firm number from a photo exists to win the signature before the yard is seen; the correction arrives later, when switching installers costs you a deposit. One of the oldest red flags in this trade is a price given without seeing the yard, and it survives because the correction is always explainable: "look what we found." A good installer finds it first — standing in your yard, before anyone signs. If a company will quote you exact from a photo, ask them to put "no change orders for site conditions" in writing, and listen to the silence.

**Can I at least get a ballpark without an onsite visit?**
Yes — that's what the range is for. Most AJ Turf installs run $9–$15 per square foot depending on the turf chosen and the site. That range is honest over the phone or from a photo. What's not honest is pinning an exact figure to a yard nobody has measured.

**What does the free onsite estimate actually cost me — anything?**
Nothing but the time of the visit. You leave with one exact written price and the product and method named. There's no obligation attached to it, and the payments only start if you sign: 30% to schedule, 35% at install start, 35% on completion.

**My yard is simple and flat. Doesn't that make a photo enough?**
Flat is exactly the case where a photo misleads most. Flat Southwest Florida lots are where drainage has to be deliberately designed into the base — and "looks flat" from above says nothing about where water stands in August, what's under the sod, or how material reaches the yard. Simple-looking yards are where the inexpensive surprises live.

**What if two installers measure onsite and a third quotes lower from a photo?**
Then you don't have three quotes for the same job — you have two prices for your yard and one price for a photograph of it. Compare the two measured quotes spec for spec, and ask the third for their number after they've seen the yard and put "no site-condition change orders" in writing.

**How soon can the install happen after the onsite quote?**
Installs are typically scheduled within about two weeks. And because the price was measured on site, the schedule doesn't come with the satellite quote's usual sequel — a mid-job renegotiation when the crew finds what the photo couldn't show.
