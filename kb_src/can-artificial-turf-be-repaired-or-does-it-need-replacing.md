# Can artificial turf be repaired, or does the whole yard get replaced?

**By Jereme Strange, AJ Turf** — years on the manufacturing side of the turf industry, and years installing and repairing turf in Florida.

**The answer:** Most artificial turf damage can be repaired without replacing the whole yard — seams can be re-glued, wrinkled sections re-stretched, melted or torn patches cut out and replaced, and contaminated base remediated underneath lifted turf. Replacement is the honest answer only when the product itself, or the base under most of it, has failed.

## Start with the diagnosis, not the verdict

Turf almost never fails all at once. It fails at a seam, an edge, a traffic lane, or a stripe where the sun hits it through a window — and each of those failures has a different cause and a different fix. A company that walks in and says "replace it all" before checking which layer failed is selling, not diagnosing.

The field checks are simple. Tug the seam: a clean adhesive release means the glue bond failed and the seam can be re-done; torn backing means that section needs a patch panel. Check the edges for movement: wrinkles that worsen at midday usually mean the perimeter has crept loose. Get down to ground level where the dog goes: odor that is strongest at the ground and survives cleaning lives in the base, not the grass. And look at the pile direction from two sides — panels laid facing different directions read as two different lawns, and no amount of brushing fixes that.

## What repairs actually look like

AJ Turf's repair work falls into three shapes, from smallest to largest:

| Repair shape | What it covers |
|---|---|
| Seam, ripple, and missing-turf repair | Open or visible seams re-glued on seam tape with a full spread of adhesive and cure weight; ripples re-stretched and re-secured; small missing or damaged sections patched |
| Ripple and missing-turf repair | Wrinkling and damaged areas corrected together — re-stretch, re-secure the perimeter, replace the sections that cannot be saved |
| Full reinstall | All-new turf, seams, and concrete borders — built to the full AJ Method, not a patch standard |

Within those shapes, the common fixes are consistent. An open seam gets re-glued on seam tape, weighted for the full cure window. Wrinkles from a loosened, spike-secured perimeter get re-stretched and re-secured properly — on our work, that means the edge rebuilt as a glued concrete border instead of spikes put back in the same sand. A window-reflection melt patch gets cut out and replaced — but only after the reflection is dealt with, because a patch alone will melt again in the same stripe. Pet odor that lives in the base gets the honest construction fix: lift the turf, remove and replace the contaminated base material, and re-lay over a clean base with a proper pet infill stack.

## The three DIY mistakes we get called to undo

A large share of repair calls trace back to the same three things. First, **not knowing turf must be seamed** — no seam tape, no glue, or no idea that seaming is an inch-by-inch process — which shows up as open, visible seams. When the pieces and the base are sound, that is a seam repair, not a replacement. Second, **not knowing turf is directional** — pieces laid mismatched or facing the wrong way read as two different colors and never brush out. The fix is to lift and relay the offending panel in the right direction; our rule is pile leaning toward the house in a backyard and toward the street in a front yard. Third, **spiking the perimeter** — spikes through turf work loose under heat and foot traffic, lift, and can injure. The fix is rebuilding the edge the way it should have been built: a poured concrete border with the turf glued to it.

## When replacement really is the answer

Sometimes it is. If the turf itself is a budget product with a perforated backing that never drained a yard properly, no cleaning or patching fixes the architecture — the product is the problem. If the base has failed across most of the yard — washing out, dipping, and rippling everywhere rather than in one spot — you are not looking at a repair, you are looking at a rebuild. And if the yarn itself is spent in the traffic lanes, flat and worn past what power-brushing and fresh infill can restore, an honest installer tells you it is product life, not a fixable defect.

## Florida note

Florida manufactures its own repair list. Heat makes turf expand and contract daily, which is what creeps spike-secured edges loose and opens seams that were glued without cure pressure. Low-E windows reflect concentrated heat — over 200°F in the reflection path — and melt geometric stripes into otherwise healthy turf; that damage is excluded from manufacturer warranties, and it is repairable only if the window gets film or screening first. And in pet yards, our heat and humidity turn a urine-saturated base into an odor problem no surface treatment reaches. All three are everyday Southwest Florida calls, from Fort Myers and Cape Coral to Naples, Estero, Bonita Springs, Sanibel, and Lehigh Acres.

> **The honest downside**
> Repair is not always the cheap door. Base remediation means lifting turf and replacing contaminated or failed material underneath — construction, with a construction price. A patch will also rarely be invisible in every light: a new section next to sun-faded, worn turf can read as a slightly different shade until it weathers in, and pile direction has to match or it will announce itself. And some damage is a symptom — re-gluing a seam without fixing why it opened, or patching a melt stripe without filming the window, just schedules the same repair twice.

> **The gotcha**
> Watch for two opposite pitches. The first: "it all has to come out" — a full replacement quoted for what is a seam re-glue or an edge rebuild. The second: a cheap patch quoted for what is actually a failed base, which fails again within a season and gets blamed on the turf. The question that separates diagnosis from salesmanship is the same one we would ask: "Which part failed — the seam, the edge, the base, or the product — and how did you check?" If they cannot answer layer by layer, they are guessing with your money.

## FAQ

**Can a separated seam be fixed without replacing the turf?**
Usually, yes. If the backing is intact, the seam can be re-glued on seam tape with a full spread of adhesive and weighted through the cure — that is the most common repair we do. If the backing itself is torn, that section gets a patch panel instead. Either way, it is a repair, not a new yard.

**My turf melted in a stripe near a window. Can that be patched?**
Yes — the melted section is cut out and replaced. But the reflection has to be fixed first, with anti-reflective film or an exterior screen on the offending glass, or the patch melts in the same stripe. Melt damage is also excluded from manufacturer warranties, so it is a repair cost, not a claim.

**The whole yard ripples in the afternoon and flattens by morning. What is that?**
That pattern usually means thermal expansion working against a loose perimeter — most often spike-secured edges that have crept. The fix is to re-stretch the turf and re-secure the perimeter properly, then top the infill back up to spec. On our repairs, the edge gets rebuilt as a glued concrete border so it stops moving.

**How much does a repair cost?**
It depends entirely on which layer failed, which is why repairs start with a free onsite assessment rather than a phone number. We diagnose it in person, quote the exact price on site, and there is no minimum job size for repair work.
