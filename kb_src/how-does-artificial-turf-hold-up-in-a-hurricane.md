## Start with what nobody can tell you

There is no hurricane performance standard, wind-uplift rating, or published test result for a residential artificial turf installation. Not for our installs, not for anyone's. Florida's statewide turf rule requires that edges and seams be anchored, at minimum against wind and flooding — it requires the anchoring to hold, and it does not certify a wind speed, publish a test, or rate any product for a storm.

So this page will not tell you turf is "hurricane-proof," "rated to" some number of miles per hour, or guaranteed to survive a named storm. No honest installer can. What can be discussed — carefully — is construction logic: how a yard is held down, and which way of holding it down makes more sense when the ground is saturated and the wind is pulling at the edges.

## The construction logic

Wind doesn't usually take a whole yard at once. It works at edges and seams — the places a surface can be lifted, peeled, or shifted. That's why the anchoring method is the storm conversation.

**Spikes in saturated sand.** The standard industry method drives nails or spikes through the turf around the perimeter. Each spike is a single point of hold in the ground — and in Southwest Florida the ground is sand. Saturated sand holds a spike worse than dry sand, spikes already work loose under normal thermal cycling and foot traffic, and a spike that pulls out of turf is a failure point and a hazard in ordinary weather. In a storm, a spiked perimeter is a row of individual points waiting for the weakest one to let go.

**A glued continuous perimeter.** The AJ Method pours a 3×3×3-inch concrete border around the full inside perimeter and glues the turf edges to it with turf glue carrying a 15-year warranty. No spikes or nails ever go through the turf — they secure only the woven weed barrier and the seam tape underneath it. The edge stress is distributed along a continuous bonded border instead of concentrated on points driven into sand.

**Infill ballast.** On top of that, the whole surface carries infill at 1.5–2 lb per square foot, brushed in against the grain. Across a yard, that is a substantial, evenly distributed weight holding the surface down — ballast a spiked, lightly infilled install doesn't have.

Put together: a glued continuous perimeter plus full infill ballast is a materially better wind-uplift story than spikes in saturated sand. That is a statement about construction, not a performance claim — and it's as far as the evidence honestly goes.

## The other storm: water

Hurricanes in Southwest Florida are as much a water event as a wind event. The turf system is built to drain: a screening-sand base compacted to 95% Standard Proctor (ASTM D698), subgrade graded for positive drainage, and permeable turf over the top. Florida's rule also carries the guardrails — no pooling or increased stormwater runoff to neighboring properties, and a 10-foot setback from any waterbody unless a physical barrier like a seawall exists. A yard built that way is designed to shed and drain storm water through the system rather than hold it on the surface. Flooding beyond the system's design — storm surge, a yard under standing water — is a different event, and no drainage design claims to defeat it.

## Florida note

Hurricane season runs June 1 through November 30, and it's the season this construction logic exists for: saturated sand is the normal condition of a Southwest Florida yard in a storm, not an edge case. It's also why installs pause around storms — and why post-storm damage is one of the things that drives repair calls in the fall. If a storm does lift an edge, open a seam, or shift a panel on any install, that's repair work: seams can be re-glued on tape, edges re-bonded, and panels re-set — starting with a free onsite assessment.

> **The honest downside**
> Turf is not warranted against hurricanes, full stop. Acts of God are excluded from the manufacturer's warranty, and AJ Turf's 1-year labor warranty is not storm insurance either. A major storm can damage a well-built yard — flying debris, fallen trees, surge, and flooding don't respect construction methods — and storm damage is a repair bill, not a warranty claim. If your buying decision needs a guaranteed storm outcome, no turf product can honestly give you one.

> **The gotcha**
> "Hurricane-rated" turf. There is no such rating — no test, no standard, no published data behind the phrase wherever you see it advertised. It's the storm-season version of every other invented number in this industry. When an installer leans on hurricane claims, ask one question: "Rated by whom, tested how, and where's the data?" There won't be an answer, because there isn't one. Judge the construction instead: how are the edges held down, and what's the ballast?

## FAQ

**Is artificial turf hurricane-proof?**
No, and no installer can honestly claim otherwise. There is no wind rating or published test data for residential turf in a hurricane. What's controllable is construction: glued continuous edges and infill ballast instead of spikes in saturated sand.

**What part of a turf yard fails first in a storm?**
The edges and seams — they're where wind gets under a surface and where water undermines a perimeter. That's exactly why the anchoring method matters more than any claim about the turf product itself.

**Does the turf warranty cover hurricane damage?**
No. Acts of God are excluded from the manufacturer's warranty, which covers UV stability and strength of the product. AJ Turf's warranty is a 1-year labor warranty on the installation. Storm damage is handled as repair work.

**My turf lifted at one edge after a storm. Does the whole yard need replacing?**
Usually not. Edge lifting is one of the standard repair shapes: the edge can be re-bonded to the border, seams re-glued, and panels re-set. Repairs start with a free onsite assessment, and there's no minimum job size.
